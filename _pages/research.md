### Job Market Paper
Formulary Design and Consumer Choices in Medicare Part D.
<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    Health insurers use formulary tiering and exclusion to steer patients across competing 
    prescription drugs, and manufacturers pay rebates for formularies that favor their products. 
    This paper studies vertical contracting between Medicare Part D plans and insulin manufacturers 
    and the implications for consumers. I model formulary design as a multi-principal, multi-agent 
    game in which manufacturers offer rebate contracts to insurers that are contingent on formulary 
    choices. I first estimate consumer demand for Part D plans and long-acting insulins and construct 
    insurer and manufacturer payoffs under alternative formularies. I then solve for equilibrium 
    formulary choices and rebate contracts. I evaluate Medicare Part D policies that expand access to 
    insulin: (1) prohibiting exclusion, (2) requiring coverage of biosimilar insulin, and (3) capping 
    patient out-of-pocket (OOP) costs. These policies generate small gains in consumer surplus through 
    broader formulary coverage or lower OOP costs. However, these restrictions weaken insurers' leverage 
    in the contracting game, resulting in lower manufacturer rebates and higher net drug costs for 
    insurers—average rebates fall from 29% under the status quo to 6% when exclusion is prohibited. These 
    results highlight a tradeoff between consumer access and the cost of prescription drugs.
  </p>
</details>

### Working Papers
Formulary Restrictions and Drug Rebates: 
Evidence from Medicare's Protected Drug Classes (with Alex Olssen and Christopher Adams).
<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>Selective contracting with healthcare providers is a common tool used to control costs. 
    In the context of pharmacy benefits, health insurers often design restrictive drug
    formularies and negotiate rebates from drug manufacturers in exchange for preferable 
    formulary placement. However, formularies for Medicare Part D plans cannot exclude
    drugs in six “protected” classes. We evaluate the price effects of this policy by 
    comparing Part D rebates with rebates in the commercial insurance market, where insurers
    have more freedom to exclude drugs from their formulary. Our analysis leverages detailed 
    administrative data on the universe of discounts in Part D, Medicaid, and 340B at the 
    drug-plan-year level from 2012 to 2020. We combine detailed data on rebates across all 
    major government programs with data on aggregate net discounts from SSRHealth to back out 
    estimates of rebates for outpatient drugs in commercial health insurance. Our estimates 
    suggest that policies that restrict the formularies that insurers can use result in modest 
    effects on drug prices, insurer costs and federal spending in the Part D program.
  </p>
</details>
